Welcome to the Flora of the Saanich Peninsula

The Saanich Peninsula is part of the homeland of the W̱SÁNEĆ people, whose knowledge of its plants and landscapes extends back countless generations. Among the region’s published botanical references, Szczawinski and Harrison’s (1972) Flora of the Saanich Peninsula remains a classic. Harvey Janszen devoted much of his life to revising this work but passed away before completing it. Local naturalists have since carried his efforts forward, and this website shares that continuing legacy.

To date, 1,105 vascular plant species have been recorded on the Saanich Peninsula, but many have not been seen in recent years. Some may be lost to us—overlooked, forgotten, or waiting to be rediscovered. Others may be lost because of us, through habitat change and invasive species.

We’re bringing together local naturalists and community members to search for these “lost” plants, update the historical flora, and better understand how biodiversity is changing across the Saanich Peninsula.

Explore the map and tabs above to discover which species may still be out there, and join the search through the “Lost” Plants of the Saanich Peninsula project on iNaturalist.

Community contributions to our knowledge of the regional plant diversity

Through the combined efforts of 7416 individuals, the Saanich naturalist community has now documented a total of 1105 vascular plant species. Based on search efforts over the last decade, 85 percent of the historically reported plant diversity has been confirmed and 168 new species reports added to the list. Community members have now documented 72 percent of the plant diversity known to the region on iNaturalist.

You can contribute to this growing record of the regional plant diversity by submitting your observations to the “Lost” plants of the Saanich Peninsula iNaturalist project. Here, you can browse photos of some of the most commonly documented plants, as well as recent observations and top observers across the region, based on iNaturalist data. Extirpation Risk

Which plants may have disappeared from the Saanich Peninsula? This tool gives a first indication from the pattern of past sightings alone, applying one component of our protocol for detecting local extinction (Simon et al., 2025).

Extirpation probability (EP) runs from 0 (likely still present) to 1 (likely lost locally). Using Solow’s (1993) method, it compares when a species was sighted with the whole period of record, from the earliest collection in our data (1859) to 2025. EP is highest for plants that were recorded repeatedly and then stopped being seen. Plants recorded only once or twice tend to score nearer the middle, since so few records say little either way.

To explore the results:

  • Tick a species in the table, which lists the highest EP first, to map its records and see its Regional EP, based on all of its sightings together.
  • Each circle on the map is a group of sightings treated as one population, coloured by its EP. Hover over a circle to see its Cluster EP.
  • Population radius sets how close sightings must be to count as one population. A smaller radius can reveal sites where a plant has gone unseen even though it persists elsewhere.
  • Use Filters to narrow the list, for example to native species.

A high EP is a reason to go looking, not proof of loss. It does not account for where or how hard people have searched, or for suitable habitat, which the full protocol goes on to weigh.
